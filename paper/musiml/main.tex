\title{What Counts as a Mistake? Annotating Recitation Events\\in Quran Memorization Transcripts}

\author{Anonymous Author(s)}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
An application that checks Quran recitation has to separate a genuine error from the ordinary business of reciting from memory: catching a breath and repeating three words, restarting an ayah, correcting oneself mid-word, opening with the isti'adhah. We report a completed human annotation of 100 production recordings under a taxonomy that makes that separation explicit, covering 348 scored units and 162 localized events across ten combined labels, and we freeze an executable scorecard for it. Two existing systems, a plain diff and the segmentation-and-repair pipeline of a deployed application, reach a localization F1 of 0.93 and 0.79 while reaching a label-aware F1 of only 0.53 and 0.45. Both score zero on eight of the ten labels, and the diff calls the opening formulas errors 46 times. The open problem in recitation checking is not finding textual differences but naming them.
\end{abstract}

\section{The distinction the task turns on}
Quran memorization is normally practised with a listener who corrects the reciter. An application that stands in for that listener transcribes with commercial ASR and then compares the transcript against the reference. Edit distance answers a question nobody asked. In our production data 74\% of reviewed ayah units carry no event at all, and among those that do, a substantial share are things a human listener would never mention: the reciter repeated a phrase after a breath, said a word wrongly and immediately fixed it, or used a written form differing from the Uthmani orthography without differing in what was recited. An application that reports these loses the learner's trust faster than one that occasionally misses a real slip.

We therefore annotate transcript-against-reference differences as typed, localized \emph{events}, and we judge the text rather than the speaker: a label records what differs between transcript and reference, not whether the reciter or the ASR caused it. Unwritten vowel and tajweed errors lie outside a transcript-only rubric and outside this work.

\section{Taxonomy and annotation}
\textbf{Combined labels.} An earlier pilot used two parallel fields, an event type and a benign/mistake verdict, plus an \texttt{uncertain} escape hatch. Reviewing real recordings made both untenable: the verdict was never independent of the type in practice, and \texttt{uncertain} absorbed exactly the cases that needed a decision. The rubric now uses one combined label per event, \texttt{substitution\_mistake}, \texttt{omission\_mistake}, \texttt{insertion\_mistake}, \texttt{substitution\_corrected}, \texttt{omission\_corrected}, \texttt{repetition\_benign}, \texttt{letters\_benign}, \texttt{spelling\_benign}, \texttt{basmala\_benign} and \texttt{isti3adha\_benign}, with \texttt{clean} for a reviewed unit carrying no event. A unit reviewed and found clean is not the same as a unit not yet reviewed, and the data distinguishes them.

\textbf{Four decisions that took adjudication.} \emph{Omissions} include missing beginnings, interiors and endings, with no exemption for the final ayah of a recording: stopping early still leaves material the reciter did not produce, and a reviewer cannot infer intent from recording position. \emph{Repeats} span the original phrase together with its extra occurrence against a single reference copy, aligning their localization with corrected events; a wrong-then-correct attempt is a corrected event, while a correct-then-incorrect restatement is a \texttt{substitution\_mistake} spanning both attempts, with the order recorded in the note. \emph{Spelling} differences removed by an agreed context-independent normalization receive no event, and only a residual form needing contextual acceptance receives \texttt{spelling\_benign}; the accepted contexts are enumerated rather than generalized, so the category cannot quietly become blanket final-letter deletion. \emph{Localization} uses verbatim words and half-open spans into the original whitespace token arrays, with an empty hypothesis span anchoring an omission and an empty reference span anchoring an insertion.

\textbf{Scale and composition.} All 100 selected recording cases are human-approved: 314 ayah units, 233 of them clean, 115 within-ayah events and 47 opening-formula annotations, over 274 ayahs in 38 surahs. The event distribution is uneven by nature, from 62 \texttt{substitution\_mistake} down to one \texttt{insertion\_mistake} and one \texttt{omission\_corrected}, and any evaluation has to survive that. Ten learner identities contribute, one of them 46 recordings. Selection was purposive, so these counts describe the annotated set; they do not estimate error prevalence or imply generalization to unseen reciters. Three further approved recordings are held as reserves outside the scored set. We verified identifiers and transcripts against both source exports and excluded punctuation-normalized duplicates and redundant clean subpassages. A deterministic 30-record sample covering every label, including the singletons, is available to reviewers.

\section{An executable scorecard}
Systems receive the reviewed ayah split, the ayah ids and the exact references, so detection and segmentation are supplied rather than scored; that task is treated in a companion submission. Per unit a system returns events of the form \texttt{\{label, hyp\_span, ref\_span\}}, with half-open spans into the transcript and reference token arrays.

\textbf{Opening formulas are scored.} Each record's isti'adhah and basmala are presented as their own unit, with the formula as the transcript and an empty reference; a compound opening yields two events over one token array. Deciding that a basmala preceding the ayah is benign rather than an insertion is exactly the judgment under test, and excluding it would hide the failure mode that turns out to matter most.

\textbf{Matching.} Predictions and gold events are paired one-to-one within a unit by maximum total similarity, so a single broad prediction is credited with at most one gold event and the remainder count as misses. A pair is eligible only when \emph{both} spans overlap, $\min(\mathrm{sim}_{hyp}, \mathrm{sim}_{ref}) \ge 0.30$; averaging the two, as an earlier version did, lets an exact reference span carry a match while the hypothesis span points somewhere else entirely. An empty span is an anchor, matching another anchor within one token and a real span when it falls inside it. Pairing ignores labels, which is what allows localization to be reported apart from naming.

\textbf{Scores.} The primary measure is label-aware event F1: a paired prediction is a true positive only when its label also matches, and a mislabeled pair counts once as a false positive and once as a false negative. We report micro F1 as the headline, macro F1 so the rare labels cannot be ignored, and localization F1 from the same matching with labels dropped. An application-facing cost is reported separately in raw event counts, $(2\,\text{missed} + \text{false flags})$ per 100 units, so that the stated exchange rate of one missed mistake for two unnecessary flags is the one the formula implements. Predicting nothing gives micro F1 0; the gold annotation gives 1. Seven oracle tests run against the real annotation before any system is scored, including four that an earlier scorecard passed perfectly while being wrong: mislabeling every mistake, omitting every benign and corrected event, fabricating benign events on clean units, and localizing every event to the wrong place.

The gap between the localization and label columns is the finding. A plain diff pairs 93\% of the gold events and gets barely half of their labels right. The deployed pipeline, which already models repetition and restart explicitly, does worse on both, because its repair step deletes the material an annotator marks: cleaning the transcript before comparing destroys the evidence for the benign event.

The per-label breakdown is starker than the aggregate. The diff reaches F1 0.94 on \texttt{omission\_mistake} and 0.85 on \texttt{substitution\_mistake} and scores exactly zero on the other eight labels. It emits 46 \texttt{insertion\_mistake} predictions where the annotation contains one, because it reads the isti'adhah and the basmala as inserted text. Neither system ever proposes \texttt{repetition\_benign}, \texttt{spelling\_benign}, \texttt{letters\_benign} or either corrected label. Macro F1 near 0.17 is what two working categories out of ten looks like.

This is a useful negative result for anyone building such an application. Engineering effort in a recitation checker typically goes into alignment quality, and alignment quality is not the binding constraint: both systems already find the differences. What neither does is decide what a difference means, and that decision is what the learner experiences.

\section{Limitations and what comes next}
The annotated set is purposive and small, one learner contributes 46 of the 100 recordings, and the label distribution is heavily skewed, so macro F1 here is a coverage check rather than a stable estimate. More consequentially, all 100 selected transcripts already appear as inputs in a public release of the same recordings, and twelve appear in an earlier machine-labeled pilot. The adjudications and the selection membership are private, but the inputs are not, and runtime isolation cannot establish absence of prior exposure. Any claim about unseen inputs requires a separately collected corpus; a read-only production check has since identified 63 further recordings, 57 textually distinct from the released export, as the start of one, subject to reuse permission and de-identification.

The annotation and the scorecard exist to support a measured experiment we have not yet run: supply an agent with an unlabeled development pool, the reference and the label definitions, let it produce its own annotations and an algorithm, and score only the resulting algorithm against the private set. Agreement with self-generated labels would not be independent annotation accuracy, and the score assesses the combined process rather than any single annotation's contribution. Budgets, comparison conditions and permitted feedback remain to be frozen. Gold answers, selection membership, reviewer files and annotation-session memory stay outside every agent runtime, and shared documentation carries aggregate findings and synthetic examples only.
